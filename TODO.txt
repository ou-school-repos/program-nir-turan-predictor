• It’s in the proof of Theorem thm:double_cover in paper/sections/07_bipartite_double_cover.tex:18. The exact combinatorial identity is the sentence at paper/sections/07_bipartite_double_cover.tex:25: for a connected tree T, every homomorphism T
  -> H lifts in exactly two ways to T -> B, hence
  [
  \Hom(T,B)=2\Hom(T,H).
  ]
  The Lean formalization of that identity is LeanLeontovich/LeanLeontovich/DoubleCover.lean:172, theorem connected_hom_doubleCover_eq_two_mul.

  On near-path sufficiency: it is still open in the paper. The current explicit statements are in paper/sections/03_analytical_properties.tex:94 and paper/sections/08_conclusion.tex:14. The strongest thing you have analytically right now is the
  long-spider obstruction: if all three spider legs go to infinity, that asymptotic challenger cannot beat the path. That kills the easiest non-near-path family, but it does not settle bounded gadgets near an endpoint or in the interior.

  My read is:

  - Full finite-n near-path sufficiency is probably hard and may even be false.
  - The asymptotic version for primitive targets looks plausible.
  - A realistic proof route is a gadget-factorization theorem: show that along long spines, the Perron projection makes separated defects contribute multiplicatively, then prove the minimum gadget score is attained by a path-plus-pendant gadget.
  - The current paper is already pointing at that gap: bounded rooted gadgets still need a separate minimization argument, and bipartite targets need a parity-class version.

  So yes, there is a way this could be proved, but not with the machinery currently finished in the paper. The next real theorem would be something like:

  1. Prove asymptotic factorization for rooted gadgets on primitive targets.
  2. Reduce all asymptotic challengers to minimizing a single rooted gadget score.
  3. Show that minimizer is always a near-path gadget.

  That would be a substantial result. It would not automatically give the finite-n conjecture.

› can these results be proven analytically


• Some can, some probably need computational certificates.

  Likely Analytically Provable

  - m1 = 2 base case: yes. The polynomial identity
    -c3((c1-c2)^2+c1+c2) proves the P_5 versus E_5^(2) sign. A universal
    all-threshold/all-source-tree argument remains outstanding.

  - T(7,1,9) permanent crossover: yes. The quotient has a small spectral form; once the sign flips and the subdominant ratio decays monotonically, permanence is analytic.
  - Double-cover lemma: yes. Pure homomorphism lifting argument.
  - B(x,y) spectral family: yes. The 2x2 quotient gives exact eigenvalues and likely an analytic infinite-family theorem.
  - H18 walk-table/margin: the existence at n=17 is exact finite arithmetic, but can be written as an analytic calculation from the quotient matrix.

  Maybe Analytically Provable, But Needs Work

  - Strongness of T^(1,28,1,36): probably. Need a spectral decomposition / recurrence proof showing the leading coefficient for E_n is smaller than for P_n, then separately handle pre-asymptotic sign.
  - H* as 15-vertex Leontovich: existence is finite exact arithmetic. Minimality below 15 likely remains computational unless you derive structural inequalities for all smaller bipartite pattern vectors.
  - The n=17340 even crossover: existence/threshold can be exact recurrence arithmetic; “no earlier even crossover” is computational unless you prove monotonicity over the recurrence tail and check a finite prefix.

  Hard / Probably Computational

  - “No graph on m <= 11 is Leontovich”: very hard analytically because it quantifies over all target graphs and all source trees. Current near-path filter is not enough.
  - “No smaller bipartite depth-2 graph exists”: likely computational enumeration unless reduced to a tractable finite symbolic inequality.
  - 76-vertex leaf-pruning minimality: computational certificate / exhaustive family proof, not naturally analytic.
  - Perturbed non-bipartite construction: could become analytic if you explicitly derive the quotient matrix after perturbation and prove its leading coefficient ratio is < 1.

  Best paper strategy: present finite exact computations as certified arithmetic lemmas, not pretend everything is hand-analytic. Then reserve “theorem” for claims whose quantifiers are fully covered by proof or exhaustive certificate.
